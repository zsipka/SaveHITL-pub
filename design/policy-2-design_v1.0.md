## 2. The policy

Take the 4,457 alerts of file month 7 (the data's months are numbered from 0). The whole month is one batch (Paper p. 12). For a team of 10 reviewers and a given capacity (the share of the 4,457 alerts the team can review, from 0 to 1). What each team does with an alert is specified only by the trees of section 2.1; this section defines what the trees use.

1. **Capacity.** Every alert is routed for review. K = floor(capacity × 4,457), computed in exact integer arithmetic with the capacity held as a fraction.
2. **Review order.** For each seed, one random order of the 4,457 cases; the trees take the alerts in this order. The same order is used at every capacity.
3. **Dealing.** Positions in the order are dealt to the team's 10 slots in blocks of 10, with a fresh random order of the 10 slots in each block. Each slot reviews floor(K/10) or floor(K/10) + 1 cases (at capacity 0.75: K = 3,342; two slots review 335 cases and eight review 334). The slots therefore have equal capacities (Paper p. 9), and the dealing is nested across capacities.
4. **Sharing.** Within a seed, every team and every team version uses the same order and the same dealing. A, B and C give each reviewed case to the same reviewer.
5. **Generator form.** One generator per seed, seeds 1 to 5: NumPy's legacy RandomState generator (Mersenne Twister), seeded with the seed number, used in this order:
   - first, a random permutation of the 4,457 cases. Its entry at position p is the index, in the case order of section 7 (ascending case identifier), of the case at review position p (p = 0 to 4,456). The first K positions are reviewed.
   - then a table of 446 rows of 10 uniform random numbers on [0, 1), filled row by row. In each row, the positions of its 10 numbers taken in ascending order of value (the position of the smallest number first) give a fresh random order of the 10 slots; the rows are joined and the first 4,457 entries kept. Review position p goes to the slot at entry p; positions 10b to 10b + 9 form block b, so each block receives a fresh random order of the 10 slots.
   - Slot j (0 to 9) is the team member with the j-th smallest twin number i, sorted as numbers, not as text.
   - Any other reading (for example the permutation's entry at p read as the rank of case p, or the rank of each draw in place of the sorting order) follows the same distribution but gives a different allocation, so only this form is used. Its exact call is written to every run record. P2-C1 (T7) re-draws the order and the slots from the recorded call and checks that they equal the export exactly.
6. **Decisions and their coding.** Three decisions per case, each coded 1 = alert confirmed (application rejected), 0 = alert dismissed (application accepted):
   - **D-Exp:** the decision of the twin who reviews the case.
   - **D-Mdl:** the decision of Classifier *h* (policy 1's decision).
   - **D-Fnl:** the team's final decision, the one that is scored, given by the team's tree (section 2.1).
   - **The deference draw** (team B's tree; "below 0.8" means strictly below). It is one uniform number in [0, 1) per case and seed, from its own random generator, separate from the review order and dealing; it is shared by every B version and every capacity. Form: 4,457 uniform random numbers on [0, 1) from NumPy's legacy RandomState generator (Mersenne Twister) with seed 100 + seed, seeds 1 to 5, so stream seeds 101 to 105, distinct from the review order's (1 to 5) and the bootstrap's (2026). Element i belongs to the i-th alert in ascending case identifier (the case order of section 7), so it does not depend on the review order, the dealing, the capacity or the version. Every alert gets a draw, including cases where the twin and the classifier agree and cases beyond capacity, where it has no effect. The draws are exported with the allocation, so that T2 can be re-checked from the exports, and written to every run record (section 11).
7. **Beyond capacity:** as the trees. Exceeding capacity changes nothing in how the reviewed cases are decided.
8. **No dependence on λ.** The twins' decisions and the deference draws are fixed, and policy 1 applies its 0.5 rule at every cost ratio. Decisions are therefore replayed once per team instance, capacity and seed, and costed at each λ.

### 2.2 What follows from the trees (arithmetic, not results)

- At capacity 0, every team equals "confirm every alert". At capacity 1, no team has overflow.
- The same order is used at every capacity, so the reviewed set at a lower capacity is contained in the reviewed set at a higher one (nested).
- B differs from A only where B's tree ends in the classifier's decision against the expert's.
- On every case, C's decision is at least A's; C differs from A only where C's tree ends in the bias rule.

**Team B, from the trees.** On every reviewed case, team B takes either the twin's decision or the classifier's. On each reviewed case, with d = 1 if the case's deference draw is below 0.8 and d = 0 otherwise (d is defined on reviewed cases only), on expected loss and accepted frauds, case by case: B − policy 1 = (1 − d) × (A − policy 1), and B − A = −d × (A − policy 1). So agreement can only move B towards automation on the cases it reviews. Without a review cost, B can do worse than policy 1 only through the unreviewed cases (the same for every team at a given capacity and seed), or if the twins themselves do worse than the model on the reviewed cases; in that second case agreement reduces the damage. B's loss avoided per review (section 6) is about 0.2 × A's on the same reviewed cases, by construction, so any review cost above that break-even makes B worse than automation.

## 3. What is used

In line with the project outline's aim to minimise re-training (Method), nothing is retrained. The only new data are the unbiased twins, produced by the framework's own generator.

| Element | What |

The twins keep the stored experts' target costs, α and other weights. ## 4. The three teams

How each team decides an alert is set out in [section 2.1](#21-the-teams-behaviour), with one diagram per team: [team A](#team-a-behaviour-diagram), [team B](#team-b-behaviour-diagram) and [team C](#team-c-behaviour-diagram).

Each team version has 10 reviewers (Paper p. 8). Every team is run over the whole capacity grid of section 5. There are 15 team instances: A, B and C, each in five versions.

**What a version is.**
- **The twins are generated once.** One generator run produces all 50 twins (section 3). Nothing is regenerated between versions.
- **A version is one fixed set of ten twins, drawn at random.** The 50 twins are split into five disjoint teams of ten by one call, made once and never repeated with another seed: a random permutation of the twin numbers 0 to 49 from NumPy's legacy RandomState generator (Mersenne Twister) with seed 42. Version r (1 to 5) is the ten twins whose numbers are the permutation's entries 10(r−1) to 10r − 1. Every twin is in exactly one version, and all 50 are used. No constraint is placed on the draw. Version r is "drawn team r". The call, the seed, the software version and the five lists in slot order are written to a draw record, which every run reads.
- **Teams A, B and C are not different experts.** Within a version, all three teams have the same ten twins. The teams differ only in the rule that sets the final decision on a reviewed case (section 2.1). So each team is run on all five versions, which gives 15 team instances.
- **The whole process runs for each version:** 5 seeds × 22 capacities. Each allocation of cases is applied three times, once with each team's rule.
- **Why there are five versions.** Results may depend on who is on the team.
  - Comparisons are always made within a version. The experts are the same and only the rule differs, so a difference comes from the rule alone (section 7, "Pairing of versions").
  - The five versions repeat the experiment with different experts, and the five-version rule (section 7) decides how their results combine.
- **What the five-version rule shows.** The five versions have no twin in common, and together they hold all 50. "All five versions do worse" (or beat) therefore means that five separate groups of reviewers, covering the whole pool, each give that verdict on their own. The versions are still not independent experiments: they share the alerts, the review order, the deference draws and the resamples, and all 50 twins come from one generator run with one distribution of target costs (section 3). So the rule shows that a verdict does not depend on which of the 50 twins are on the team. It does not show that the verdict would hold for another pool of reviewers. The mean of the five versions gives each of the 50 twins the same weight, because the ten slots of a version review equal shares (section 2, item 3). The 5% family-wise level remains approximate (section 7).
- **Seeds and versions repeat different things.** Seeds repeat the random parts: the review order, the dealing and the deference draws. Versions repeat the experts.

The synthetic reviewers were generated to a target expected cost of 0.035 per case at λ = 0.057, on average (Paper p. 7). The author, from domain experience, considers this realistic for experienced subject matter experts; the project outline makes the author's experience in the domain a stakeholder input (Method).

The decision rules, the bias against applicants aged 50 or over, the realistic range and the capacities at which each team is judged are this study's choices. Each team implements one of the project outline's three teams:

| Team | Project outline team it implements | Reviewers | Capacity at which it is judged (section 5) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A, base (control)** | "a standard mixed team" | Five versions: drawn teams 1 to 5 | 1, all 4,457 alerts reviewed: the control. A at 0.75 is the comparator in comparisons 4 and 5 only |
| **B, agrees with the model** | "a team whose members largely agree with the model score". Here the team takes Classifier *h*'s decision on about 4 in 5 reviewed disagreements (section 2.1) | The same five versions as A; within a version, the same 10 twins as A | Every capacity of the realistic range, 0.65 to 1 plus 10/11, against policy 1; 0.75 against A |
| **C, biased** | "a team whose capacity is materially exceeded and is biased against certain applicant attribute(s)". Here the attribute is age, 50 or over (section 2.1) | The same five versions as A; within a version, the same 10 twins as A | Every capacity of the realistic range, 0.65 to 1 plus 10/11, against policy 1; 0.75, which this study takes as capacity materially exceeded, against A |


Facts that bear on reading the teams (their behaviour is in section 2.1):
- **Only C is biased.** The twins' age weight is exactly 0, and every other weight and α equal the stored expert's (check P2-G (b)). C's bias comes only from its rule. An age weight of 0 does not guarantee a zero age gap: other weighted features may be correlated with age, and the intercepts are refitted. The twins' remaining gap is measured on months 3 to 6 and on month 6 and reported (P2-G (d), P2-T1).
- **B adds no bias of its own.** Any age gap it has is carried by B and by policy 1 alike and is reported as the model's. The classifier is not retrained.
- **B agrees by a set probability, not by observed behaviour.** The 0.8 in team B's tree is the author's domain judgement; no source gives it. B's measured agreement with the classifier is reported, not set: it is higher than 0.8 because a reviewer already agrees with the classifier on many cases. B and A have the same reviewers on the same cases.
- **C's rule only adds rejections** (a consequence of C's tree). It raises false rejections of legitimate applicants aged 50 or over, and lowers missed frauds among applicants aged 50 or over. Its net effect on expected loss has no fixed sign.
- **No pressure effect.** Overload acts only through the cases beyond capacity. Expected from the rules: as capacity falls, C's measured age gap shrinks while wrong rejections at every age rise; the study reports bias diluted by overload, not accentuated (section 9).
- **Built to the same cost.** Each expert's expected cost per case at λ = 0.057 was set from one distribution, target mean 0.035, standard deviation 0.005, clipped to 0.02 to 0.046 (Paper p. 7). The twins keep the stored target costs and are fitted on months 3 to 6. Expert error rates may deviate from their targets outside the fitting period (Paper p. 5). The fit holds the expected rates over months 3 to 6; in month 7 the fraud share among alerts is higher (0.16, against about 0.115), so the realised rates are expected to drift from the targets, and they also differ by the random draw.

## 5. The sweep

**Definition** as in policy 1 design section 5. Policy 2 has two settings, capacity and cost ratio λ, plus seeds for the review order and dealing. The sweep is kept in full.

**Capacity.** 0, 0.05, 0.10, …, 0.95, 1 (21 values) plus 10/11: 22 values.
- K = floor(capacity × 4,457) at every grid point.
- 10/11: K = 4,051, labelled "10/11 of the alerts (4,051 reviewed)". 10/11 is the share the benchmark gives its experts (Paper p. 9). Part of the realistic range.
- **Realistic range:** capacity 0.65 to 1, plus 10/11, i.e. the 9 grid points 0.65, 0.70, 0.75, 0.80, 0.85, 0.90, 10/11, 0.95 and 1. Teams B and C are judged against policy 1 at every one of them (comparisons 2 and 3, section 8.2). Team A is the control at capacity 1. The team-against-team comparisons 4 and 5 are at 0.75, which this study takes as capacity "materially exceeded", the project outline's words for its biased team. Results below 0.65, and A's results below 1, are descriptive.
- **Why the range, and not the whole sweep, decides.** The realistic range is this study's choice of realistic capacity levels, and the deciding comparisons are made only there; the results below 0.65 are kept in the full sweep and are descriptive. At capacity 0, for example, no alert is reviewed and every team confirms every alert: in point value this costs more than policy 1 at λ = 0.057 (next bullet), but it accepts no fraud, and its age gap is 0 only because every applicant among the alerts is rejected, so it is not a fairness gain (section 6, "A property of the age gap").
- **Why a boundary can exist.** At capacity 0 every team is "confirm every alert", which at λ = 0.057 costs more than policy 1 (from policy 1's error counts on the test month, rule P2-L: 3,746 × 0.057 ≈ 213.5 against 2,878 × 0.057 + 40 ≈ 204.0). At capacity 1, A and C review everything with twins built to the cost target of section 4. The sign of the difference against policy 1 can therefore change between capacity 0 and capacity 1. Where it changes, per team and per measure, is the boundary (P2-B). For B, the reviewed-cases part comes only from the reviewed cases where B keeps the twin's decision against the classifier's (section 6).

**Cost ratio.** λ ∈ {0.035, 0.0537, 0.057, 0.171}, the values at which policy 1 is costed (policy 1 design, section 5). Decisions do not change with λ (section 2, item 8); only expected loss changes. The main comparisons are at λ = 0.057 only. How results away from 0.057 are read: rule P2-U.

**Seeds:** 1 to 5. Each seed fixes one review order, one dealing and one set of deference draws.

**Size:** 22 capacities × 5 seeds × 15 team instances = 1,650 replays; costed at 4 ratios, 6,600 cells.

## 6. What is measured

Per team instance, capacity, λ and seed, on the month-7 alerts. The three risk measures (expected loss, regulatory exposure, fairness exposure) exactly as in policy 1 design section 6.

| Measure | Definition | Project outline link |
|---|---|---|
| **Expected loss** | Σ over cases of [λ·1(rejected, legitimate) + 1(accepted, fraud)]; total and per case | Risk axis 1 |
| **Regulatory exposure** | Count of accepted frauds; miss rate = accepted frauds ÷ all frauds among the alerts, with the share-stopped sentence. The share-stopped sentence states the miss rate the other way round wherever it is reported, in the form "x% of the frauds among the flagged applications were let through, that is, (100 − x)% were stopped". Lower is better. Verdicts are on the count; the rate is reported, not judged separately | Risk axis 2 |
| **Fairness exposure** | Age gap (false rejection gap): false positive rate aged 50 or over minus that under 50, among legitimate alerts. Closer to zero is better. Verdicts on the absolute value; checks fixed by construction on the signed value. Both are reported | Risk axis 3 |
| **Split of the difference: reviewed and unreviewed cases** | Applies to expected loss and to accepted frauds. Policy 2 can differ from policy 1 only on two kinds of case: those the team reviews, where the team decides instead of the model, and those beyond capacity, where every alert is confirmed instead of the model deciding. The difference between policy 2 and policy 1 is therefore the sum of two parts:<br>• **reviewed-cases part:** on the cases the team reviews, the team's result minus policy 1's result;<br>• **unreviewed-cases part:** on the cases beyond capacity, the result of confirming every alert minus policy 1's result.<br>The two parts add up exactly to the total difference, in every seed. The split shows whether a team does worse than policy 1 because its reviewers decide worse, or because the cases it cannot review are rejected. For team B the reviewed-cases part comes only from the reviewed cases where B keeps the twin's decision against the classifier's. When two teams are compared at the same capacity, the unreviewed-cases parts are equal and cancel, because both teams leave the same cases unreviewed.<br>**Five versions.** Rules P2-R1 and P2-R3 judge the two parts of the mean of the five versions, each with its 95% interval. Within a seed the unreviewed-cases part is the same for every team and version, because they share the overflow; only the reviewed-cases part differs between versions. Each version's two parts are also exported and shown | Separates the two failure conditions |
| **Override rate** | Share of reviewed cases the team accepts, overturning the alert; also the share of reviewed cases where the team's decision differs from Classifier *h* (for B, also the share of disagreements on which B deferred, expected near 0.8; its denominator is the reviewed cases where the twin and the classifier disagree, in the resample) | Monitoring indicator |

**Seeds.** Each per-case result is averaged over the 5 seeds before resampling. The minimum and maximum over seeds of each point difference are reported beside the interval.

**Ratio indicators in the bootstrap.** Override rate, loss avoided per review and subgroup disparity on reviewed cases are ratios. In each resample the denominator is the number of reviewed cases in that resample (the sum of the seed-averaged reviewed shares of the resampled cases), not K. At capacity 1 the two coincide.

**Indicators that are not defined everywhere.** At capacity 0 (K = 0), load, loss avoided per review, override rate and subgroup disparity on reviewed cases are reported as "not defined". Subgroup disparity on reviewed cases is also not defined where an age group has no reviewed legitimate case; in the bootstrap, such resamples are dropped for that indicator and counted, as in G3 (section 7). These indicators carry no verdict, so a drop is only reported.

**A property of the age gap (arithmetic from the definition).** Beyond capacity every legitimate case is rejected in both age groups. The cases beyond capacity are drawn at random, so they add false rejections to both groups at about the same rate, and they pull the gap towards the reviewed cases' gap scaled by the capacity. A smaller gap at lower capacity can therefore come from rejecting more applicants of both groups. Wherever policy 2 is closer to zero on the age gap than its comparator while its overall false rejection rate is higher, it is reported as "gap narrowed by rejecting more applicants in both groups", not as a fairness improvement.

## 7. Comparison method

- Cases in ascending case identifier; every export joined by case identifier.
- **Paired case bootstrap.** On each resample, the measure is computed for both sides of a comparison on the same resampled cases, and the difference is taken (first named minus second named). For the age gap, the absolute gap of the first minus the absolute gap of the second, each recomputed per resample from decision, label and age group. Policy 2's per-case input is the seed average (section 6).
- **One index matrix** of B = 50,000 resamples, drawn once, in one call, from NumPy's legacy RandomState generator (Mersenne Twister) with seed 2026, shared with policy 1's own intervals and with policy 3: 50,000 rows of 4,457 random integers from 0 to 4,456, filled row by row, as 64-bit integers (another integer type would give other draws). Row r is resample r; each entry is the index of a case in the case order above. Policy 1's first 1,000 rows (G1) are checked at the build to equal a fresh draw of 1,000 rows of 4,457 from the same seed. The run record holds the generator name, the call and a checksum.
- **G1.** Every policy 2 interval uses all 50,000 resamples, so that each edge of a deciding interval rests on about 20 resamples (section 8.2). Policy 1's own intervals (B = 1,000, policy 1 design section 6) use the first 1,000 rows of the same matrix.
- **G2.** Interval endpoints are percentiles with linear interpolation between order statistics: the q-th percentile (q as a fraction) sits at position (n − 1) × q of the n sorted resamples, counted from 0, and a position between two order statistics is interpolated linearly between them. n is B, or fewer for the age gap where G3 drops resamples. The software version is in the run record.
- **G3.** A resample where one age group has no legitimate case leaves the age gap undefined on both sides at once. It is dropped for the age gap only, counted and reported. If more than 1% of B (500 resamples) are dropped for a comparison, its age-gap verdict is not given.
- **Zero threshold.** An endpoint with absolute value at most 1e-9 is treated as 0. Reason, from the definitions: 1e-9 lies above floating-point rounding of a sum over 4,457 cases and below the smallest real difference (one case changed in one seed moves per-case expected loss by at least λ/(5 × 4,457) ≈ 1.6e-6 at λ = 0.035).
- **Verdict words** (per measure; measures are not combined):
  - **does worse:** lower endpoint above 0;
  - **beats:** upper endpoint below 0;
  - **no difference shown:** otherwise.
  For comparisons against policy 1, "does worse" means policy 2 does worse than automation. In a team contrast, it means the first-named team does worse.
- **Interval level.** The 61 deciding intervals (section 8.2) are Bonferroni-adjusted: 1 − 0.05/61 ≈ 99.918% each, with interval ends at the percentiles 2.5/61 % and 100 − 2.5/61 %. The calculation uses these exact values (0.040984 % and 99.959016 % to six decimals, computed at full precision); rounded values such as 0.0410 % appear in labels only. Every other interval is 95% and labelled descriptive.
- **Five versions of A, B and C.** Each version's difference is computed within the same resample and judged with its own interval, at the level of the comparison. **Does worse** if all five versions do worse; **beats** if all five beat; otherwise the verdict of the mean of the five versions' differences, with its own interval from the same resamples. This gives two routes to "does worse" (all five versions, or the mean of the five), so the 5% family-wise level of the Bonferroni adjustment is approximate, not exact. For the age gap, each version's difference uses the absolute gap. Decisions are not pooled across versions. Each version is also shown.
  - **Pairing of versions.** In comparisons 4 and 5, version r of B (or C) is compared with version r of A, which has the same twins.
  - **Where the five-version rule applies.** The rule above applies to the 61 deciding results, for team B as for A and C. Every other team-level verdict (P2-B (b), P2-T4, P2-T5, P2-F7, P2-L, P2-U, section 9) is descriptive: each version gets its own 95% verdict, and the team's verdict is that of the mean of the five versions at 95%, without the five-version rule (the descriptive verdict and the deciding verdict may differ).

## 9. Bias × capacity: compound or cancel

**The question.** Does team C's bias compound or cancel when capacity is exceeded? Comparator: A. Capacity exceeded: capacity 0.75; adequate: capacity 1.

**Measure.** For each metric m in {signed age gap; count of false rejections of legitimate applicants aged 50 or over; expected loss at 0.057}, per version and for the mean of the five:
I = [m(C, 0.75) − m(A, 0.75)] − [m(C, 1) − m(A, 1)], where m(team, capacity) is the metric for that team at that capacity, with a paired bootstrap interval (descriptive, 95%).
**Compound:** I above 0. **Cancel (in part):** I below 0. **Additive:** otherwise.

**What follows by construction (section 2).** C and A share the reviewers, the review order and the overflow, and C's rule does not depend on capacity. So C − A at any capacity is the sum of C's rule changes over the reviewed cases. Reviewed sets are nested, so the changes at 0.75 are a subset of those at 1. Hence, for the signed age gap and for the count, I ≤ 0 in every seed, version and resample: the bias can only be diluted by overload. For these two metrics the result is a check, labelled "by construction", not a finding. Expected size, from the random order: about 0.75 × (C − A at capacity 1). For expected loss, I is minus the sum of the rule changes that fall beyond capacity at 0.75 (λ for each legitimate case, −1 for each fraud); its sign is not fixed, and it is the only part of this test that is a finding.

**Against the expectation line.** The observed C − A gap at each capacity is plotted against capacity × (C − A gap at capacity 1), with the seed range. The expectation rests only on the random order, so a deviation is read against the seed range, not the case-bootstrap band. It is descriptive and supports no verdict.

## 13. Limits

1. Reviewers are synthetic: one fixed draw of decisions per twin and case. No fatigue, time pressure or learning is modelled; exceeding capacity changes only the cases beyond capacity. This study's criterion 4, that the reviewer has the information and time to disagree with the model ([policy 3 design, section 1](policy-3-design_v1.0.md#1-what-this-policy-is-for)), is observable only through the override rate.
2. One alert-generation model, one test month: 4,457 alerts, about 711 frauds (policy 1 design, limit 1). Frauds the Alert Model does not flag are outside the study.
3. Cost ratio implied, not observed (policy 1 design, limit 2).
4. Away from 0.057, neither policy 1 nor the synthetic reviewers are re-optimised (P1-U; policy 1 design, limit 3; P2-U). How much better a re-tuned policy 1 would do there is not measured.
5. The reviewers are regenerated twins, not the stored experts. They are made with two changed settings: the age weight (0) and the fitting months (3 to 6). A consequence of the fitting months: each twin keeps its stored expert's target cost, but the generator re-draws how that cost splits between false positive rate and miss rate. The comparisons between policies are not affected, because every policy and team uses the same twins. This study does not establish how realistic the twins are; it characterises their behaviour on months 3 to 6 and on month 6 alone (P2-G (d)) and reports it. An age weight of 0 does not guarantee a zero age gap; the remaining gap is measured and reported.
6. B's agreement comes from a deference probability of 0.8 set by the author, not from observed behaviour. No source gives the value; other values would give other results for team B.
7. C's bias is a rule (section 2.1). It is not a model of how bias arises in people.
8. The whole month is one batch, and alerts are taken in a random order that does not use the alert score. The policies differ in who decides each alert and how, not in which alerts come first. Sorting the alerts by score, for example reviewing the highest-scoring first, would make which alerts are reviewed below full capacity depend on the score instead of on the policy, so the policies would no longer be compared like for like. The alert score still enters as information that the experts and the router use.
9. The intervals cover which cases fell in the test month, not the policy's own randomness; the seed-to-seed range is reported beside them. The five-version rule gives two routes to "does worse", so the 5% family-wise level of the deciding results is approximate (section 7).
10. The capacity grid has a step of 0.05: a boundary is located to within that step.
